Choose CAD drafting when the job is mainly 2D and fairly contained: a tenant fit-out, a permit set for a small building, as-built plans or a run of details. Choose BIM when the building is complex, several consultants need to coordinate, the design will keep changing, or the owner wants a model at handover. Many projects sensibly use both. The rest of this article explains how to decide in a few minutes, what each route costs over the life of a project, and the questions architects ask most often about switching.

What each one actually is

CAD drafting means drawing lines, arcs, hatches and text that represent the building, usually in AutoCAD or a similar program. A plan, a section and an elevation are separate drawings. If a window moves, someone updates each drawing that shows it.

BIM (building information modelling) means building a digital model out of real objects, such as walls, floors, doors and stairs, in software like Revit. Plans, sections, elevations and schedules are views of that one model. Move the window once and every view and schedule updates.

The difference is not 2D versus 3D. It is separate drawings versus one coordinated source of information.

Where CAD drafting is the better choice

  • Small, well-defined projects. A single-storey extension or a tenant fit-out rarely repays the effort of building a full model.
  • Details and standard drawings. Many firms keep construction details in CAD even on Revit projects.
  • As-built and existing drawings. Redrawing old PDFs or scanned sheets is classic CAD work. See our PDF to DWG conversion guide.
  • Permit sets for simple buildings. A clean, well-organised CAD set is perfectly acceptable to most building departments. Our permit drawing set checklist lists what reviewers look for.
  • Consultants who work in CAD. If the rest of the team sends DWGs, a CAD-based workflow can be simpler.

Where BIM pays for itself

  • Design changes. On a building with 60 sheets, moving a core or a stair in CAD means chasing the change through every plan, section and schedule. In Revit it updates once.
  • Coordination. Structure and services clash in models before they clash on site.
  • Schedules. Door, window and finish schedules come straight from the model, so they match the drawings.
  • Existing buildings. Renovation of a complex existing building often starts with a point cloud. Our guide to scan to BIM explains how that becomes a model.
  • Clients and owners who require it. Some public owners write BIM into their requirements. The New York State Office of General Services, for example, published BIM Guidelines in January 2025 naming Autodesk Revit for authoring and Navisworks Manage for coordination, and making each consultant responsible for its own software and licences.

"Is AutoCAD dead? Should every firm switch to Revit?"

This question comes up constantly on architecture forums. The honest answer: BIM is now the norm for architects, but CAD has not gone anywhere. The NBS Digital Construction Report 2025, as reported by the RIBA Journal, found 74 per cent of architecture professionals use BIM, and 88 per cent of all respondents either use it or plan to. That survey is UK-based, so treat it as a signal rather than a US count. The flip side is that roughly a quarter of architecture professionals in that survey do not use BIM, and plenty of successful small practices still deliver in CAD.

So the better question is not "which is better?" but "which is better for this project, this team and this client?"

What it really costs

At the start

CAD is cheaper to start. You open a drawing and begin. BIM front-loads the work: a template, a family library, a model structure and staff who know how to use them. For a firm making the switch, the first Revit project is usually slower, not faster.

Over the life of the project

BIM tends to win back that time when change happens. Every revision round, every consultant update and every schedule regeneration costs less. The longer and more complex the project, the bigger the payback. On a short, stable project the payback may never arrive.

Staffing

The tool you choose matters less than having people to run it. Deltek's 2026 Clarity study of 896 US and Canadian architecture and engineering firms found firms forecasting 9.5% net revenue growth for 2026 but staff growth of only 1.2%, with turnover above 13%. Whichever route you choose, plan who will produce the drawings when the workload peaks.

Can you mix CAD and BIM?

Yes, and most firms do. Common hybrid set-ups:

  • Revit model for plans, sections and schedules, with CAD details linked or redrawn as drafting views
  • CAD survey or consultant drawings linked into Revit as backgrounds
  • A light Revit model for design and visuals, with the permit set finished in CAD on small jobs

One caution from the same New York OGS guidelines: link CAD files rather than importing them, and keep CAD imports inside Revit families to a minimum, because they slow models down. Clean content matters, which is why Revit families are worth getting right.

A quick way to decide

Answer these five questions. If you say yes to three or more, lean towards BIM.

  1. Is the building more than a simple single-use space?
  2. Will structural or MEP consultants need to coordinate with you?
  3. Do you expect major design changes after the first drawings go out?
  4. Does the client, owner or contract ask for a model?
  5. Will the building be renovated or managed using this information later?

Mostly no? A well-run CAD set will serve you well, and it will cost less.

Quick answers

What is the main difference between CAD and BIM?

CAD produces separate drawings made of lines and text. BIM builds one model from real building objects, and the drawings and schedules are views of that model, so they stay consistent.

Is BIM better than CAD?

Not always. BIM is better for complex, changing, multi-consultant projects. CAD is often faster and cheaper for small, simple or detail-heavy scopes.

Is Revit harder to learn than AutoCAD?

Usually, yes. Revit asks you to think in building elements and relies on good templates and families. Drafters who know AutoCAD can pick up the basics, but productive Revit work takes standards and practice.

Can a permit set be done in CAD?

Yes. Building departments review the drawings, not the software. A complete, clear CAD set is fine for most projects unless your client or contract requires BIM.

Can CAD drawings be converted into a Revit model?

Yes. A modelling team uses your DWG plans, sections and elevations as a base and builds the model on top. The cleaner and more consistent the drawings, the faster the model.
