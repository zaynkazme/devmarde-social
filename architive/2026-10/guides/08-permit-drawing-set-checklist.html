A permit drawing set for a US house, addition or small commercial job usually needs a cover sheet, a site plan, existing and proposed floor plans, elevations, at least one building section, structural plans and details, and energy and code notes. The exact list is set by your local building department, not by a national rule, so download its checklist before anyone starts drafting. Most rejections come from missing items and drawings that disagree with each other, and both are easy to catch before you submit.

Start with your building department, not a template

Most US jurisdictions base their residential rules on the International Residential Code (IRC) from the International Code Council, and commercial rules on the International Building Code. But each jurisdiction picks its own edition and adds local amendments. That is why one county asks for things another never mentions, and why Fairfax County, Virginia requires the code year used for the design to be written on the plans.

The model code sets the baseline. ICC training material for residential plan reviewers, based on the 2021 IRC (ICC, 2023), summarises the core rule in section R106.1.1: drawings must be clear enough to show where the work is, what it is and how much of it there is, and show in detail that it meets the code, as judged by the building official. R106.2 asks for a site plan showing new and existing structures and their distances from lot lines.

So before drafting, find three things on your department's website:

  • The submittal checklist for your project type (new house, addition, alteration, deck, ADU, tenant improvement)
  • Sheet size, scale and file format rules for digital uploads
  • When drawings must be signed and sealed by a licensed architect or engineer

The core checklist

These items appear on most US residential and small commercial checklists. Use them to plan; your local list wins wherever the two differ.

1. Cover or title sheet

  • Project address, parcel or lot number, and owner
  • A clear scope of work in plain sentences
  • Sheet index listing every drawing
  • Codes and editions used, occupancy, construction type, number of stories and floor areas
  • Designer contact details, and space for a stamp where one is required
  • Deferred submittals, such as truss drawings that follow later

2. Site plan

  • Property lines with dimensions, north arrow and scale
  • Existing and proposed structures, with distances to lot lines and between buildings
  • Setbacks, easements, driveways, drainage and utilities as required
  • Flood zone, protected trees or historic designations if they apply

3. Floor plans

  • Existing plans and proposed plans, or one plan with a clear legend for existing, to be removed and new
  • Overall and room dimensions, room names and uses
  • Door and window tags tied to schedules
  • Stairs, egress windows and exit routes
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide alarm locations

4. Elevations, roof plan and sections

  • Every side of the building, with heights, grade lines, materials and roof pitch
  • Roof plan with slopes, drainage and roofing
  • At least one building section showing floor and ceiling heights, insulation and assemblies
  • Wall sections and typical details where the reviewer needs to see how it is built

5. Structural sheets

  • Foundation plan, floor framing and roof framing plans
  • Beam, header and post sizes, and connections between new and existing work
  • Wall bracing: braced wall lines, methods and panel locations
  • Engineer's calculations and manufacturer data where the design goes beyond prescriptive tables

6. Energy, MEP and supporting documents

  • Energy compliance path, insulation R-values and window U-factors
  • Mechanical, electrical and plumbing plans where the jurisdiction requires them with the building permit
  • Soils reports, truss shop drawings, product evaluation reports and special inspection forms as applicable

How three real jurisdictions differ

Here is what the same kind of job looks like in three places. None of these is a national standard; each applies only in its own city or county.

JurisdictionWhat stands out
New York City, NY (Department of Buildings)Strict sheet format: 24 x 36 inches minimum, 36 x 48 maximum, 8-point minimum text, a DOB job number and page number on the lower right of every sheet, and a space for the examiner's stamp. Sets without the design professional's seal are rejected.
San Mateo, California (Building Division)One checklist covers new homes, additions, alterations and ADUs. It adds California items: CALGreen green building checklist, energy forms printed and signed on the plans, protected tree locations, and state water-efficiency (WELO) forms.
Fairfax County, Virginia (Land Development Services)Residential plans at 1/4 inch = 1 foot minimum, designer name and address, the code year, a wall bracing method and worksheet, insulation values and truss shop drawings. Plans by a Virginia architect or engineer need an embedded digital signature, seal and date.

The New York rules come from the DOB's plan exam guidelines (revised July 2024). They are written for the Next Day Review pilot in Manhattan, but they show what DOB examiners expect, and the DOB states that an incomplete set will not be examined at all; it is marked Incomplete in DOB NOW. The California items come from San Mateo's residential plan submittal checklist (revised March 2025), and the Virginia items from Fairfax County's building plan minimum submission requirements and its residential addition and alteration page.

The lesson is simple. A set drawn perfectly for Fairfax could still bounce in New York on sheet size alone.

Why permit sets get sent back

Reviewers write comments when they cannot confirm something meets the code. Fairfax County publishes its common residential plan comments, and the topics are telling: wall bracing, emergency egress, smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, garage separation, safety glazing, stair protection and crawl space ventilation. Most are not design problems. They are missing information.

Other comments that come up again and again:

  • Dimensions that do not agree between plans, elevations and sections
  • Existing and new work drawn the same way, so the reviewer cannot tell what changes
  • Sheet numbers and detail references that do not match the index
  • Text that is unreadable because sheets were reduced or badly scanned

When comments come back, answer every one in writing and cloud the changes on the revised sheets. Fairfax asks applicants to respond to each open comment before resubmitting.

Who stamps the drawings

Whether a set needs a stamp depends on state law and the type of work. The IRC itself says drawings must be prepared by a registered design professional where the jurisdiction's statutes require it. In many places a designer or the owner may draw simple residential work, while structural changes and commercial work more often need a licensed architect or engineer. Ask your building department early.

Where a stamp is required, a professional licensed in that state reviews the drawings, takes responsibility for them and seals them. A drafting studio can prepare the full set for that professional, but it cannot replace them. If you already have an architect or engineer of record, they stay in charge of the design and the seal.

A ten-minute check before you submit

  1. Print or open your department's checklist and tick it against the actual sheets.
  2. Check that every sheet in the index exists, and every detail reference points to a real detail.
  3. Pick three key dimensions and confirm they match on plan, elevation and section.
  4. Preview the PDF at the required sheet size and make sure the smallest text is readable.
  5. Confirm seals, signatures and dates are on the sheets that need them.
  6. Ask someone who did not draw the set to read it cold.

Quick answers

What drawings do I need for a residential building permit?

Usually a cover sheet, site plan, floor plans, elevations, a building section, structural plans and energy notes. Your local building department's checklist sets the final list.

Can I draw my own permit plans?

In many places, yes, for simple residential work. Structural changes, larger buildings and commercial projects often need a licensed architect or engineer. Check with your building department.

Do permit drawings need to be stamped?

Only where state law or the jurisdiction requires it. When they do, a professional licensed in that state must review, sign and seal them.

What scale should permit drawings be?

There is no single national answer. Fairfax County, Virginia, for example, sets 1/4 inch = 1 foot as the minimum for residential plans. Your local checklist will say what it expects.

Is one national permit checklist enough?

No. Codes are adopted and amended locally, so sheet sizes, forms and required details vary. Use a general list to plan, and your local list to submit.
